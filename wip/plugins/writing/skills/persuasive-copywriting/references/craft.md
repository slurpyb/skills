# The Craft — Andy Maslen's *Write to Sell*

Where the tactics tell you *what* to say, the craft tells you *how*. Copywriting is a
**craft, not an art** — learnable, drillable, checkable. Almost every rule below ends in
a concrete test you can apply to a draft.

## KFC — plan before you write
Turn a vague goal ("more sales") into concrete objectives for the *writing*. Ask three
questions before writing a word:
- **K — Knowledge.** The facts the reader needs to *justify* buying. Easy; almost everyone gets the K.
- **F — Feel.** The emotional state you want the reader in — *this is where buying decisions are made,* and the part almost no one reaches for. A good F-statement is **unprovable**: "Excited to be our customer," "Worried they're missing out," "Reassured we're trustworthy," "Envious of existing customers." If a client answers the "what should they feel?" question with a fact ("that we offer 2,000 profiles a month"), push back — that's not a feeling.
- **C — Commit.** The exact action: "Buy now," "Recommend a friend," "Block out two days." Be specific and direct, never vague.

The K and C are easy; the **F is where the craft lives.**

## Benefits vs Features
The single biggest shift to becoming a good copywriter: lead with the **B-word**
(benefits), not the **F-word** (features). The reader will *tell* you they care about
features — nobody buys without checking them — but **benefits make the sale.**

> People want to know **WHY** they should buy, not **WHAT** they're buying.
> **Benefits make the sale. Features let the reader rationalize the decision afterward.**

Writers default to features because the people who make the product are too close to it
(engineers love engines). Meet the *need* and you make the sale: sell Jim hand-stitched
brogues on the promise they make him attractive, and he buys the shoes.

**Drill:** list a product's features in a left column; translate each into a reader
benefit on the right. Paperclip "weighs <1 g" → "no extra postage." "Made of steel" →
"won't break and lose your documents." Lead with the benefit, supply the feature as proof.

## AIDCA — the killer structure
AIDA + **Conviction** (because we live in sceptical times). The structure for every piece
of copy, online or offline:

**A — Attention.** The headline / subject line does this job. See below.

**I — Interest.** The reader cares about **WIIFM — "What's In It For Me?"**, not your
product. Answer "how will my reader's life improve?" in the first sentence. Use the
**FAB ladder**:
- **Feature** — "comes with bi-xenon headlamps" (the fact)
- **Advantage** — "shine 30% further than halogen" (why it's superior)
- **Benefit** — "you're safer on the road at night" (the difference to *their* life)

Benefits come in three types — **NIB**: **Noble** (what they'll admit being swayed by),
**Immediate** (small wins every product should give — free delivery), **Basic** (what
*really* motivates them but they won't admit — "we make you look good"). Be **specific**:
not "you'll save money" but "saves this office manager $3,250 a month." Specificity beats scepticism.

**D — Desire.** Interest ≠ wanting. Two levers: (1) **Bring it to life** — put the reader
in a story starring themselves using the product (Maslen wanting a classic car so badly
"it was already mine" — he could smell the leather). (2) **Restrict supply** — a cut-off
date or limited numbers ("we must limit attendance to 120"). The "eBay effect": nobody
likes to miss out.

**C — Conviction.** They still aren't buying — caution or suspicion. Convince them the
risk of buying is smaller than the risk of *not* buying: **attributed** testimonials
("Mme Marie-France DuPont, 37, Paris" — keep the customer's natural voice and minor
errors for verisimilitude), free sample/trial, reliability statistics, positive press,
money-back guarantee. Use them all.

**A — Action.** Tell them exactly what to do — **Short, Simple, Direct, Clear.** Avoid
ambiguity, wordiness, vagueness. Make it a command ("Order by January 15th"), not "If
you'd like to place an order…" Give several ways to respond. Push them off the fence.

## Headlines & openings
**4× as many people read the headline as the body** — invest your time there.
- **Combine three elements: news + benefit + curiosity.** Curiosity alone loses ("Why are freelance copywriters like dried apricots?" → "don't know, don't care"). Benefit-led wins; all three combined out-pulls everything.
- **Be specific** ("…saves this office manager $3,250 a month").
- **Keep it short** — ~10–14 words, one line. A famous insurance headline: "Cash if you die, cash if you don't" (8 words, two of them power words).
- **Never end a headline with a full stop** — a full stop says *stop reading.*
- **Write a dozen, then choose.** If you write the headline last, leave a placeholder so the top isn't blank.

**Keeping them reading — the "black arts":** broken lists (split a promised list across
paragraphs); teasers at paragraph ends ("But that's not all…"); end a page mid-sentence
so they must turn over; promise goodies later ("In a moment I'll reveal the five biggest
errors…"); give the PTO a reason ("Mrs Smith's story, overleaf").

**Six magic words:** **Easy, Quick, Free** (don't dilute with "absolutely"; state what
the free thing is *worth*), **Now, Please, Guarantee** (you can't promise it always
works, but you can promise it works *or their money back* — they hear "it will work").

**Openings:** address the reader directly, start on *their* concerns, answer WIIFM right
away. If using an image, avoid clichés (half-naked models, smiling "businesspeople,"
random animals) — show the product in context.

## Power words & sentences
**Power words** connect straight to the brain — short, often monosyllabic, many *sound*
great: Love, Hate, Sex, Cash, Risk, Care, Child, Give, Huge, Best, Win, Lose, Burn.
Hunt down floppy words in redrafts and swap in zingier ones:

| Weak | Powerful |  | Weak | Powerful |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cost-effective | Cheap | | Reliable | Rock-solid |
| Optimal | Best | | Solve | Crack |
| Upgrade | Boost | | Convenient | Easy-to-use |

- **"You" is the most powerful word** — tap the reader's self-interest. "Order now and *you* will get…" beats "…I will send you…". If you must, "I"/"we" beats the company name.
- **Use verbs, not nouns — kill "nounitis"** (nominalizations). "Our specialization is the provision of IT solutions" → "We specialize in solving IT problems." The verb is almost always shorter.
- **Prefer Anglo-Saxon over Latinate:** Before>Prior to, Let>Permit, Spread>Disseminate, Get>Obtain, Find out>Determine, Make>Construct. Holds even for CEOs — they're busier and pay *less* attention.
- **Be positive, not negative:** "Hurry">"Don't delay"; "Satisfaction guaranteed">"Your money back if not satisfied."
- **Keep sentences short** — aim for an **average of 16 words** (an *average*: mix 8-word and 24-word sentences). A score starting with 2 or 3 means find the culprit and split it.

**Orwell's six rules** (the spine of Maslen's style chapter): (1) avoid clichéd
metaphors; (2) never a long word where a short one does; (3) if a word can be cut, cut
it; (4) never passive where active works; (5) never jargon if an everyday word exists;
(6) break any rule sooner than write something barbarous. Plus: omit needless words
("at this point in time"→"now"); use adjectives to add *information*, not emphasis
("a mansion" not "a huge house"); prefer the active voice.

## Tone & punctuation
**Tone:** on the page there's no sound, so simulate tone deliberately. Default rule:
**write as you speak** — what you'd say in the pub, edited lightly. Use **contractions**
("you'll", "I've") for a conversational tone. Vary **mood** for effect: indicative
(neutral fact), interrogative (open/interested), conditional (win/win), imperative
(bossy), subjunctive (exploring). Match the language to the reader's *relationship with
the brand*, not their education.

**Punctuation is a sales tool** — its job is to **clarify meaning** (so the reader can
act), not to mark breaths.
- **Comma:** separates extra info from the main idea — one comma at the start/end, a *pair* in the middle (drop the second and the meaning shifts).
- **Semi-colon:** join two sentences that belong together, or separate list items that already contain commas.
- **Colon:** introduce a list, or when the first half promises what the second delivers. Never put a colon after "including."
- **Full stop — Maslen's favourite.** Unending sentences are the biggest block to comprehension. Watch for the **comma splice** (two sentences joined by a comma when a new subject begins). **Use full stops early and often.**

## What works / what doesn't
**Works:** (1) staying focused on the reader; (2) **brevity** ("brevity is the sister of
talent"); (3) long *interesting* copy (in direct marketing, long copy reliably out-pulls
short — what matters is how *interesting*, not how long); (4) **storytelling** (any case
study is a story); (5) **asking questions** (closed/limited/open — stack early "yes"
answers); (6) **rapport** (find a bridge; flatter; match register); (7) **fresh ideas**;
(8) judging by **efficacy, not taste**.

**Doesn't:** (1) **showy writing** (your writing is a window — they should see the view,
not the glass); (2) **jargon** (communicating status, not meaning); (3) long-windedness;
(4) **talking about yourself** ("We've finished our exciting new range" → *So what?*);
(5) **boilerplate** ("As a valued client…" implies you have clients you don't value);
(6) **over-excitement** — "exciting/amazing/unique/revolutionary" means you're *emoting,
not evoking*; write down *why* instead, and be specific; (7) **humour** ("people don't
buy from clowns"); (8) **subjectivity** ("I don't like it" misses the point — Courier
out-pulls "modern" faces in tests, but marketers won't use it because of taste).

## Readability gate
Treat readability as **pass/fail on every draft** (run `scripts/readability.py`):
- **Words per sentence (WPS)** — the single most important number. ~16 average is fine; 20s+ = find the long sentence and split it; under 12 average ≈ guaranteed comprehension.
- **Flesch Reading Ease** — higher is easier; **60 is where plain English begins.** Under 50 → almost always sentence length; split sentences, shorten long words.
- **Flesch-Kincaid grade** — ~7 means a busy, half-attentive reader can act without effort (their *effective* reading age is below their real one). It does *not* mean your audience is twelve.
- **Passive voice** — aim low (zero is often ideal), but keep a passive verb when it genuinely reads better.

*Write to Sell* itself scores **RE 66.6 / FK 7.1 / ~13 WPS / 3% passive** — Maslen eats
his own cooking. The numbers are guides, not gospel; your judgement prevails — but a
draft scoring worse than plain English almost always needs another pass.
