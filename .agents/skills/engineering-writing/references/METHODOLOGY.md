# Methodology

The full process, start to finish. Built from reading real engineering blogs end to end and real writing-craft, persuasion, and copywriting sources, not from generic advice about "good writing." Every technique below is attributed to where it actually came from, not asserted as general wisdom. Use [VOICE-AND-SLOP.md](VOICE-AND-SLOP.md)'s rules exactly.

## 1. Figure out what you're actually writing

An announcement, a how-we-built-X deep-dive, or a retrospective, these are different shapes for different reader questions, not the same post with a different label. Decide this first. Full detail in [REGISTERS.md](REGISTERS.md).

## 2. Diagnose the reader before you draft a word

What does this reader already believe, and what do they already know? A post explaining a caching layer to someone who doesn't know they have a cache problem needs a different opening than one for a reader already comparing three solutions. Write for the smallest viable audience, the one engineer with the exact problem, not a generic broad readership. Full detail in [PERSUASION-ETHICS.md](PERSUASION-ETHICS.md).

## 3. Find the remarkable thing before you write the rest

Ask, honestly, before drafting: is there an actual surprising finding, number, or turn here, or is this the same generic post everyone already wrote about this topic? If the honest answer is the latter, keep digging until you find the real one, or don't publish. This single gate catches more bad engineering posts than any sentence-level fix ever will. Chip and Dan Heath's _Made to Stick_ names what "remarkable" actually requires, not just surprising but simple, concrete, and credible, an idea that's merely unexpected without being concrete just reads as a stunt. Ann Handley's test for whether a draft is even worth finishing is a good second gate: utility, inspiration, and empathy multiply together, not add, so a post with genuine utility but zero empathy for what the reader already knows still fails.

## 4. Draft badly and fast, separate from editing

Give yourself permission to write a genuinely bad first draft. Anne Lamott's own term for it, "shitty first drafts", and her point stands on its own: clarity comes from revision, never from the first pass, every writer researched for this skill who's written about their own process says some version of this. Don't try to write the whole piece at once. Lamott's other device, the "one-inch picture frame": find one small, concrete section you understand completely and write only that, then the next one, not the whole mountain at once. Editing and cutting happen later, as their own separate pass, not while you're still trying to get the ideas down.

## 5. Choose the opening on purpose

Cold scene, concrete problem stated flat, disarming confession, rhetorical question over an artifact, myth stated then dismantled, a reframed word, credentials then the claim, a TL;DR, tension, a two-sentence tradeoff. Nine real, working patterns, pick the one that fits what you actually have, don't default to the same one every time. Full menu in [OPENINGS-AND-MOMENTUM.md](OPENINGS-AND-MOMENTUM.md).

## 6. Structure for momentum, not just organization

Every sentence's job is to get the next one read. Build the piece so a section ends with a reason to keep going, reveal a solution iteratively instead of dumping the finished version on the reader at once, show the alternatives you rejected and why, not just the one you picked. Full detail in [OPENINGS-AND-MOMENTUM.md](OPENINGS-AND-MOMENTUM.md).

## 7. Write in plain voice, be specific about everything

Second person, short sentences, one idea each. Every claim gets a number, a name, or a real example behind it, or it gets cut. No hype words, no filler openers, no em dashes, no decorative emoji. Full rules in [VOICE-AND-SLOP.md](VOICE-AND-SLOP.md).

## 8. Cut on the second pass

Read the draft and remove every word that doesn't earn its place, this is a distinct pass from drafting, done once the ideas are already down. John McPhee's method for this, worth using literally: remove words in such a way that no one would notice anything is missing, don't just lop sentences off the end to hit a length target.

## 9. Read it aloud, then run the checklist

Every source researched for this skill converges on this independently, a copywriter (Halbert), a well-known essayist, and this repo's own tone-of-voice guide all say the same thing: read the draft aloud and you'll stumble over every place that isn't smooth. Then run the actual checklist in [VOICE-AND-SLOP.md](VOICE-AND-SLOP.md) before you ship.

## 10. Close with the one thing, not a summary

End with the single thing you want the reader to do or remember, a command to run, a link to the repo, the one sentence you'd want quoted if this is the only part anyone reads. Never close by restating what you already said above.

## Where persuasion craft fits, and where it doesn't

Steps 2 and 3 borrow real, honest technique from marketing and copywriting research, diagnosing the reader, finding what's remarkable. Where that research turns to fear, urgency, status, or manufactured scarcity, it stops applying. The full borrow-and-avoid breakdown, with the reasoning for each line, is in [PERSUASION-ETHICS.md](PERSUASION-ETHICS.md). Read it before you assume a persuasion technique belongs here just because it works in advertising.
